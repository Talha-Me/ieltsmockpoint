Castle Road Run
The organising committee of the annual Castle Road Run welcomes all entrants in the event. We
hope the following details will provide you with all the necessary information to make your run as
enjoyable as possible.
Before race day you will receive a registration card containing your personal details, the race event
entered and your T-shirt size. Competitors must bring this registration card with them when they
collect their race number. All runners, except those who have completed the four previous Castle
Road Runs, will wear white T-shirts with red numbers on the back. Runners who are taking part in
their ﬁfth race will be identiﬁed with a green number instead of a red one. Please take the time to
complete the questionnaire included on the back of the registration card in order to assist us in
improving upcoming Castle Road Runs.
Entrants may change from the Full Run to the Half Run, or vice versa, up until 28th October. Please
email details of the change to us. The $20 difference in entry fee will not be refunded for those
changing from the Full Run to the Half Run, and it will be payable by those changing from the Half
Run to the Full Run.
Due to safety requirements, the Castle council has imposed a maximum number of entrants.
Entries received after this number is reached will be fully refunded.
All competitors must collect their race pack prior to the start of the event from North Lake Hotel,
12 Skelton Street, any day between the hours of 10am and 7pm. Competitors who do not come
from the Castle region, please get in touch with the Race Manager personally or by email to arrange
for collection of your race number and race pack.
Competitors may collect the race numbers and race packs of other competitors provided they
bring the registration card for each competitor they wish to collect for.

Hatcliff Backpackers
Hatcliff Backpackers’ Hostel is only a short stroll from Cathedral Square and the city centre. Shops,
buses, supermarkets and cinemas are not far away. In fact everything you’ll want to see and do in
the city is on our doorstep.
All beds and bunks are fully made. Every room has free heating, modern furniture and air
conditioning. All singles, twins, doubles and tourist ﬂats have a TV and most have an ensuite
bathroom and electric blankets. You can usually upgrade your accommodation from a single share
to a single or double (for example) and pay the difference when you arrive.
‘Guest only’ areas are secured by combination locks. Personal lockers can be hired for a maximum
of three days, or you may use our safe for the overnight storage of valuables.
Our two spacious modern kitchens are both fully equipped with everything from pots and pans
through to microwaves and even toasted-sandwich makers. Light meals are available from 11 am
until 11 at night and you can get drinks and groceries 24 hours a day.
Information is available on what to do and where to go in the Castle and surrounding regions. We
also have a launderette on site. Don’t forget to check out our notice board for cheap cars and travel
deals.
You will be greeted at the airport arrival hall by a staff representative who will transport you to our
door. We offer one free collection or drop-off per traveller and individuals can elect to use this
either upon arrival or departure. We can advise and reserve seats for you on all national
backpacker transport, coach and rail services – and every adventure activity you would like to try.
We require 24 hours’ notice to arrange your transport and book your accommodation.

Forklift operator course
The Liftsure Training Academy offers a comprehensive course for individuals wishing to be
certiﬁed as forklift operators. There are very few legal requirements to train for a job as a forklift
operator. Those that do exist are for your safety and that of the people around you. The minimum
age for operating the equipment is 17, with course completion possible at age 18.
Forklift operators are expected to be in good health, meaning that they cannot suffer from any
condition that would interfere with the alert operation of the lift truck. Trainees should recognize
that although the law makes no reference to this, screening by a future employer could involve a
health test as part of the process of getting hired.
We offer new lift operators a ﬁve-day course. The fact that our training is conducted so quickly in
no way suggests that participants are released into the workforce before they’re ready. In fact, our
accelerated approach has been proven to boost knowledge retention in the workplace.
So, what exactly will the course cover? To begin with, students will learn how the forklift’s design is
vital to its stability during operation. You will be given detailed instruction on the theory used for
the various forklift models. Along with this, there will be an in-depth explanation of the parts that
make up the vehicle.
Afternoons will be spent in our academy warehouse onboard the trucks. Driving the forklift itself
may not be problematic; however, once a load of signiﬁcant weight is added, operating the vehicle
can be a challenge for new drivers. Instruction concentrates on this area since proper load
transportation is paramount. Class sizes are kept small to allow instructors to monitor driving and
correct improper actions. Driving a forklift will also involve having a working knowledge of
dangerous goods. Classroom study will include how to recognize dangerous goods, their proper
packaging, and the immediate action to be taken when a spill occurs with such a product. Students
will also have time to work on strategies to use when driving in conﬁned spaces and narrow aisles.
It should be noted that forklifts can be dangerous pieces of machinery. Therefore, we include a
module emphasizing the need for an inspection by the operator before each shift. It includes such

=== PAGE 8 ===
things as tire pressure and signs of wear to key parts. This simple precaution is a proven way to
lessen on-the-job accidents. On completion of the course, students will sit an exam and
demonstrate the correct operation of a forklift.

The lure of diving
Scuba diving (diving with Self-Contained Underwater Breathing Apparatus) is an activity that can be
undertaken and enjoyed by practically anybody. However, you will enjoy it more if you are aware of
your own limitations, learn to recognise them and stay within them. Diving can certainly offer fun
and adventure, as long as you respect the safety rules and accept a degree of responsibility
towards your diving buddies. Diving is not a solitary pursuit; it is safer and more enjoyable when
shared with others. There is an element of risk inherent in diving as in other adventure sports, but
this risk is reduced to acceptable limits by the observation of basic safety rules and the
acceptance of the need for sound training.
Diving will make certain demands on the body, so it is important to maintain a good level of
physical ﬁtness. This does not mean athletic prowess or superhuman physical strength. The sort
of ﬁtness most useful to the diver is the ability to expend energy in a slow, controlled way, usually
keeping some in reserve for emergencies. The ﬁt diver will also suffer less from the effects of cold
or extreme weather conditions.
People dive for different reasons. For some, the opportunity to enjoy a contrast to their everyday
life is suﬃcient attraction. The undersea world is quiet; it allows movement in three dimensions; it
supports a totally different range of plant and animal life and it enables the diver to enter, for a brief
period, another medium. Many people are amazed at the profusion of life and colour found in the
seas, not just in tropical waters but also around temperate coasts. Colours will often be muted by
the ﬁltering effect of the water but can be instantly restored by using an underwater lamp. The
range of life which the diver can study underwater extends from the microscopic to the largest
mammals on earth. Diving is not restricted to coastal sites or the seas either – any appropriate
body of water can be explored by the inquiring diver. Inland fresh water locations have a quite
different nature, and often allow valuable experience to be gained closer to home before visiting
the sea. Rivers can be rich in archaeological ﬁnds, and some unique discoveries have been made in
old wells.
For other divers the fascination may be to search for evidence of past events in this least explored
part of our planet. The remains of ancient civilisations have been discovered and brought to the

=== PAGE 12 ===
surface by divers in many parts of the world, ﬁlling in gaps in our knowledge of the ancient world
and opening up many new avenues of research. Shipwrecks hold a unique fascination for the
exploring diver and also attract a variety of undersea life, making them perhaps the most enjoyable
of dives.
For many of us, the need to take a break for a while and experience new things is very strong.
Diving adds purpose to your travel and can bring wonder and adventure to a holiday.
However, there are some people for whom diving is more than just a pastime. The world’s
increasing need to exploit the resources of the sea is involving more and more people in research
under the surface in a wide range of occupations. Some will study the cycle of life and the part
each organism plays within the system, while others play a role in extracting the energy and
mineral resources that lie below the seabed.
Human beings have been diving since primitive man was forced to forage for food in the sea. In the
past, discovery of valuables from shipwrecks provided the impetus for a number of experimental
diving devices, culminating in two effective forms: the diving bell, in which divers could spend
periods submerged; and the diving helmet, invented by Englishmen Charles and John Deane of
Whitstable in 1829.
The history of sport diving can be traced more directly to the invention of the ﬁrst autonomous
diving apparatus, which did not require lines and air hoses to the surface. This new concept was
pioneered by William James, an Englishman who, in 1825, produced a workable design in which
compressed air was carried in a circular iron reservoir around the diver’s waist. We have no record
of James diving, so the credit for the ﬁrst independent apparatus that was actually used must go to
an American, Charles Condert, who built and used a horseshoe-shaped waist-mounted reservoir,
which provided a continuous ﬂow of air to a ﬂexible helmet. Condert dived with this equipment
many times in New York’s East River.
The next breakthrough was made in 1865 by two Frenchmen, Benoit Rouquayrol and Auguste
Denayrouze, whose lightweight design included a metal canister charged to a pressure of 40 bar.
The air supply was now carried on the diver’s back. Their most important invention was the
demand regulator, which enabled the diver to valve his air at the same pressure as the surrounding
water. The apparatus was marketed for sponge and wreck diving. Another important pioneer was
Commandant Yves Le Prieur of the French Navy, who was the ﬁrst to consider amateur sport diving
in the sea. He produced a lightweight, compressed-air apparatus with a regulator that was not quite

=== PAGE 13 ===
fully automatic. He initially used the equipment with goggles and later with a full face mask. In
1935 he founded the ﬁrst diving club to train with his apparatus in Paris.
